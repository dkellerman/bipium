Rumba

Three drums and a pair of sticks arguing across one clave, in Matanzas.

Read the clave lane first and count the whole thirty-two steps as one cycle. Its strokes are beat 1, the and of 2 and the and of 4 of bar one, then beat 2 and beat 3 of bar two. A son clave would put that third stroke on beat 4 instead; here it is an eighth later, and that is the rumba. Palitos run straight eighths, accented at 102 wherever a clave stroke lands and dropped to about 70 in between. The low drum takes beat 1, beat 3 and the and of 4, doubling the clave at that last position. The quinto has four strokes in bar one and six in bar two and is meant to be rewritten every cycle. Two things this grid cannot hold: the tres golpes, whose muted strokes fall between everything already here, and the fact that a real quinto player is answering a dancer, not a loop. The two hi-hat rows are unfilled because a rumba has no cymbal in it.

What it is

A patio, a set of packing crates left over from the docks, and a group of people who had no drums and made some. Guaguanco is the middle of rumba's three forms and the one that travelled: two low drums and a high one, a pair of sticks, and a clave. The salidor holds the bottom, the tres golpes fills, and the quinto darts over the top following the dancer rather than the beat. Nothing here is a backbeat. Every stroke is placed relative to five clave hits spread across two bars, and that two-bar cycle is the unit of measurement for most of the music on this page. Learn it here and salsa, songo and timba stop looking complicated.

What it sounds like

Wood, skin and voices, in a room that supplies its own reverb. The low drums are tuned loose and hit with the flat of the hand, so the bass tone is more thud than note and the open tone rings for about a beat. The quinto is cranked tight and cuts through everything, mostly as slaps. Sticks on a hollow length of bamboo give a dry knock with no pitch in it, and the clave is two pieces of hardwood that sound like a hammer on a fence post. Over all of it a lead voice and a chorus answering, recorded close and loud, with no separation between anything.

Key elements

  • Rumba claveFive strokes over two bars: beat 1, the and of 2, the and of 4, then beat 2 and beat 3. The third stroke sits an eighth later than a son clave and that one displacement is the rumba.
  • The quintoThe high drum, improvising against the dancer rather than keeping time. It plays different figures in each bar and never settles into a loop.
  • PalitosTwo sticks on a hollowed length of wood, running a constant subdivision under everything. It is the part that stops the ensemble drifting when the quinto starts pulling.
  • The bass toneThe heel of the hand into the middle of the low head, damped short. It marks the bar without ever sounding like a kick drum, because there is no attack on it.
  • Two bars, not oneThe cycle is two bars long and the two halves are not interchangeable. Putting the second half first is a real error with a name in Cuban music.

How to get them

  • Program the clave firstLay the five strokes down before anything else and leave them alone. Every other part in this style is measured against them, and moving one later to fit a melody is the fastest way to write something nobody can dance to.
  • QuintoTwo samples, an open tone and a hard slap, pitched high and gated to about 120 milliseconds. Write a different figure for each bar and resist making it symmetrical.
  • PalitosA dry stick sample with everything below 500Hz removed. Alternate two slightly different samples so the stream does not read as a loop, and keep it well under the drums.
  • Low drumTune a conga sample down three or four semitones, cut everything above 3kHz, and let the open tones ring into the next stroke rather than gating them.
  • Do not quantise the quintoNudge its strokes five to fifteen milliseconds either side of the grid by hand. The clave and the sticks stay locked; the top drum is the part that is supposed to be pulling against them.
  • Tempo184 counts the fast pulse the group counts, so one bar is four quick beats and the clave takes two bars. Halve it to 92 if you would rather see the whole cycle inside a single bar.

Reference points

Los Munequitos de Matanzas, Clave y Guaguanco, Yoruba Andabo, Chano Pozo, Carlos Embale, and the Grupo AfroCuba de Matanzas recordings. Named as reference points for the sound, not as endorsements or as anyone to imitate.

The pattern is a starting point rather than a rule. If you want the theory underneath why any of it works, Battuto measures it.
