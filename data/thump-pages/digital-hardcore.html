This is a punk drummer, sampled. The kick takes 1, 2, 3 and 4; the snare answers between them, on the and of 1, the and of 2, the and of 3 and the and of 4; the hats bash eighths above both, with the and of 4 handed to the open hat. At 192 that reads as a band playing double time, which is exactly what it was cut from, and it is why there is no Amen anywhere near this grid. A crash marks the downbeat at 110, louder than anything else in bar one except the kick. Bar two doubles the kick onto the and of 2, the and of 4 and the last sixteenth, drops a second crash on beat 3, and turns beat 4 into a three hit snare run at 110, 108 and 106. Velocities matter less here than anywhere else on the site, because the limiter you are going to put on this will flatten most of them.

What it is

Kreuzberg, 1994, and a label with a manifesto. Alec Empire had been making gabber and breakbeat records under a dozen names, formed Atari Teenage Riot with Hanin Elias and Carl Crack in 1992, and founded Digital Hardcore Recordings to release things nobody in the techno scene wanted. EC8OR, Shizuo, Bomb 20, Christoph de Babalon and Nic Endo followed. The politics were explicit and antifascist and the records were made to be shouted over. Rhythmically this sits apart from the rest of the hardcore lineage because the source material is different: where gabber built everything on a distorted 909 kick and jungle built everything on the Amen, these records are cut from hardcore punk drumming, sampled off vinyl and hammered back out at a tempo the original band could just about reach. Everything else is distortion, feedback and a guitar sample looped for eight bars.

What it sounds like

Clipping from the first bar. The drums are a punk kit sampled at low resolution, then overdriven, then limited, so the snare has no attack left and the cymbals have turned into a continuous white band across the top of the mix. Guitars are single power chords sampled and retriggered rather than played. Bass is a square wave through a distortion pedal. The vocals are shouted, doubled, and buried at the same level as everything else. There is no dynamic range by design, no reverb worth the name, and a good deal of digital clipping that nobody has tried to hide.

Key elements

  • A punk break, not the AmenThe source is a hardcore band at double time: kick on the quarters, snare on the eighths between them, cymbal riding above. That grid is why these records never sound like jungle.
  • Sampled power chordsOne chord, recorded once, chopped and retriggered on the grid. Nobody is playing a riff. The guitar is being used as a rhythm sample the same way a break is.
  • Deliberate clippingThe mix is driven past the converter and left there. Transients are gone by the second bar and that flatness is the intended texture rather than an accident of the era.
  • The crash on 1A single loud cymbal marking the downbeat. Once the distortion has flattened every transient in the kit, it is most of what still tells you where the bar starts.
  • Shouted, not rappedVocals sit in the mix as a rhythm layer at the same level as the drums, doubled and hard-panned, with no attempt to make the words intelligible.

How to get them

  • Sample a punk drummerFind a fast four-piece kit recording, take one bar, and rebuild it hit by hit at 190. Do not use a jungle break; the whole difference between this and breakcore is the source.
  • Distort in stagesOverdrive the kit bus by six decibels, then bitcrush to 12 bits, then limit. Three mild stages sound like a record; one extreme stage sounds like a broken plugin.
  • Retrigger the guitarRecord one power chord, cut it to about 200 milliseconds, and sequence it on eighths. Detune a duplicate by 12 cents and hard-pan the pair.
  • Keep the crashPut one cymbal on the downbeat near the top of the level, just under the kick. It is doing nearly all the structural work in an arrangement that has no other punctuation, so losing it in the limiter loses the bar.
  • Break the pattern every other barEvery second bar, double the kick onto the offbeats and run three snares across beat 4. At this tempo two identical bars in a row read as a stuck loop rather than a groove.
  • Mix flat and stopNo reverb, no sidechain, no automation past a mute. Set every fader once and print it. The absence of production moves is a large part of what this sounds like.

Reference points

Atari Teenage Riot, Alec Empire, EC8OR, Shizuo, Bomb 20, Hanin Elias, Nic Endo and Christoph de Babalon, plus the Digital Hardcore Recordings and Force Inc catalogues. Reference points for the sound, not endorsements.

The pattern is a starting point rather than a rule. If you want the theory underneath why any of it works, Battuto measures it.
